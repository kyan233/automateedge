How small IT support firms win new clients in 2026

By Kyan Sukhram, AutomateEdge · 2 November 2026 · 6 minute read

Short answer: most small IT support firms grow on referrals until referrals stop being enough. The firms that keep growing add one steady way of starting conversations with local businesses of 5 to 50 staff: usually a specific, useful reason to talk (like a security gap you can see from outside), followed up by phone. One new 15-person client is typically worth £8,000 to £17,000 a year, so you need very few wins for outreach to pay.

Where do small IT firms' new clients come from?

  1. Referrals from happy clients. The best clients, but you can't control when they come.
  2. Partners who see IT problems first: accountants, office-move firms, phone and broadband suppliers, and print and copier dealers. One good partner can send several clients a year.
  3. Search and reviews. "IT support near me" searches, plus Google reviews. Slow to build, but it compounds.
  4. Local networking: chambers of commerce and business breakfasts. Good for trust, slow for numbers.
  5. Direct outreach: email and phone to businesses that fit. The only channel where you choose who you talk to and when.

Most firms with 2 to 20 staff rely on the first one or two. The problem is that the people who should be doing outreach are fixing tickets all day.

Why would a business that already has IT support talk to you?

Almost every business with 15 or more staff already has someone: an IT firm, an in-house person, or "the office manager's nephew". You are not trying to replace them on the first call. Businesses change IT providers when one of these happens:

  • They're unhappy: slow replies, a different engineer every time, or the bill keeps growing.
  • They've outgrown it: one IT person is now looking after 30 people.
  • Something new lands: a customer or insurer asks for Cyber Essentials, they buy another business, or they open an office.
  • Their IT person leaves, or the contract comes up for renewal.

At any moment only a small share of businesses are in one of those situations. That's why outreach is a numbers game, and why a good opening question is simply: "Who looks after your IT, and how do you find them?"

What gets a business owner to take a meeting?

A specific, true reason that's about them, not you. Things you can check from public information, without touching their systems, include:

  • Email that can be faked. No DMARC record, or DMARC set to "monitor only". This matters most for firms that take payments by bank transfer, where fake "new bank details" emails cost real money.
  • Out-of-support software. Exchange Server 2016 and 2019 and Windows 10 all reached end of support in October 2025. A business still running its own mail server is a clear conversation.
  • Old website software: for example, a website still running PHP versions that stopped getting security fixes years ago.
  • A recent change: a new owner, a takeover, a rebrand or new bank details. Change is when mistakes and scams happen.

Offer something free and small, like a 20-minute IT and security check, rather than a sales pitch. Keep the tone calm. Exaggerated fear from a stranger sounds like a scam.

Is cold email and calling allowed in the UK?

Yes, if you follow the rules (PECR and UK GDPR). In short:

  • Email: you can email limited companies and LLPs at their business addresses without prior consent, as long as you say who you are and every email has an easy opt-out. Sole traders and partnerships are treated like individuals and need consent first.
  • Phone: check numbers against the Corporate Telephone Preference Service (CTPS) and the TPS before calling, and keep a do-not-call list.
  • Data: have a privacy notice, use only public business information, and pay your ICO data protection fee.

This is a summary, not legal advice. The ICO's guidance on direct marketing is the source to check.

How many conversations does one new client take?

Rough numbers from business-to-business calling: around 20 dials gets about 5 real conversations, and a handful of conversations gets a meeting. Then a share of meetings become clients, often weeks or months later. Your own numbers will differ, so track them from day one.

The maths still works. At typical published UK managed IT prices of £45 to £95 per user per month, a 15-person client is worth roughly £8,000 to £17,000 a year, and often stays for years.

Do it yourself, hire, or outsource?

  • Do it yourself: cheapest, but it competes with billable work and is the first thing dropped in a busy week.
  • Hire a salesperson: a big fixed cost and a long ramp-up, before you know whether it works.
  • Retainer agency: often thousands a month on a 3 to 12-month contract, and you pay whether meetings happen or not.
  • Pay per meeting: you pay only when you sit down with a real business that fits your brief. Lower risk, though you give up some control over volume.

A simple plan for the next 30 days

  1. Write down your ideal client: towns, sectors, staff numbers, and anyone you won't work with.
  2. List 50 local businesses that fit, using Companies House and their websites.
  3. For each one, find one true, specific reason to talk.
  4. Email first, then call two days later. Try three times, on different days.
  5. Book meetings within a week of the yes, and send reminders 48 hours, 24 hours and on the morning.
